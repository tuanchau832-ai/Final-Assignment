Solution options

Three ways to catch a discharge

Option A · selected

Automated sensor stations

Solar IoT stations at high-risk outfalls measure pH, dissolved oxygen, turbidity and temperature continuously and send alerts over 4G. Wireless sensor networks let water be monitored remotely, in real time and with little human effort (Pule et al., 2017).

Real time, day and nightTraceable to one outfallHigher start-up costProbes need regular cleaning

Option B

Resident test kits and an app

Trained residents test river water with simple kits and report results through an app, as in the FreshWater Watch programme, where citizen data complements agency monitoring (Hadj-Hammou et al., 2017).

Cheap to startBuilds community involvementNot continuous; misses night dischargesData quality varies

Option C

More frequent manual sampling

Inspectors visit outfalls more often with portable meters and send samples to a laboratory, the traditional method that wireless monitoring is designed to complement (Adu-Manu et al., 2017).

Accurate, legally acceptedUses existing staffLabour-heavy and costly to scaleStill hours to days behind

Design selection

Why sensor stations won

Each option was scored from 1 to 5 on every criterion and multiplied by the criterion’s weight, giving a total out of 100.

Figure 1. Weighted decision matrix. Each coloured block is one criterion’s contribution to the total.

Option A is the only option that scores highly on both of the most heavily weighted criteria, timeliness and traceability. Its weaknesses, start-up cost and probe maintenance, are addressed in the Implementation plan and Cost analysis.

The team also kept the strongest idea from each other option: from B, residents can report pollution with a photo in the app; from C, every alert is confirmed by a laboratory sample before a fine.
