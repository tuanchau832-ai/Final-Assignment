Executive summary

Every discharge, visible within minutes

Hanoi’s rivers receive large volumes of untreated wastewater every day. Only about 30% of the city’s domestic wastewater is treated (VnExpress, 2024), and factories and drains can discharge at night or in heavy rain, when nobody is watching. Inspections rely on hand-taken samples and laboratory tests that take days, so polluters are rarely traced and residents downstream are not warned.

This report proposes Hanoi WaterWatch: small solar-powered sensor stations at high-risk drain outfalls that measure pH, dissolved oxygen, turbidity and temperature every few seconds. When readings stay outside national QCVN limits, the system alerts the environment authority, turns the outfall red on a public map app and warns nearby residents. An inspector then takes a laboratory sample at that exact outfall before any fine is issued.

Sensor stations are the proposed pilot design. The comparison also includes resident test kits, manual sampling and AI-assisted IoT. A simulated prototype demonstrates the alert flow. The three-station, three-year pilot has an illustrative subtotal of US$2,731.71, using the team’s assumption of 26 routine visits per station per year. The final equipment list and service quotations are pending.

Background

Hanoi’s rivers carry the city’s wastewater. Who is affected, and why today’s tools are not enough.

The riversStakeholdersExisting solutionsCommunity voice

Context

Rivers that work as open drains

Hanoi’s inner-city rivers, the Tô Lịch, Kim Ngưu and Nhuệ, carry the wastewater of a fast-growing city. Domestic and industrial discharges are both major sources of surface-water pollution in Hanoi (Nguyen et al., 2018), and only about 30% of domestic wastewater is treated before it reaches rivers and lakes (VnExpress, 2024).

Industrial discharges add a heavier load. Factory wastewater has been linked to high COD and BOD₅ levels in the Tô Lịch, a sign of organic pollution that strips oxygen from the water (Nguyen, 2019), and zinc and chromium in the sediments of four urban rivers pose a considerable ecological risk (Phung et al., 2024). In 2024, wastewater from the Thanh Oai Industrial Cluster failed national standards, and residents reported polluted irrigation water and skin irritation (Dai Doan Ket, 2024).

The damage travels downstream. Of 15 heavily polluted monitoring points in Vietnam’s main river basins, 13 lie on the Nhuệ–Đáy system, and Hà Nam province receives 10 to 15 waves of Hanoi wastewater each year (Báo Pháp Luật, 2021).

The ideal state

What success looks like

Today

  • Discharges are found days later, if at all.
  • The polluting outfall is unknown.
  • Residents learn from the smell or dead fish.
  • Inspections are spread thin and arrive late.

With WaterWatch

  • A discharge is detected within minutes at the exact outfall.
  • An inspector is sent with a time and a place.
  • Residents are warned before they use the water.
  • Polluters pay, and open data builds trust.

Solution options

Four ways to monitor discharges

Option A · selected

Automated sensor stations

Solar IoT stations at high-risk outfalls measure pH, dissolved oxygen, turbidity and temperature continuously and send alerts over 4G. Wireless sensor networks let water be monitored remotely, in real time and with little human effort (Pule et al., 2017).

Real time, day and nightTraceable to one outfallHigher start-up costProbes need regular cleaning

Option B

Resident test kits and an app

Trained residents test river water with simple kits and report results through an app, as in the FreshWater Watch programme, where citizen data complements agency monitoring (Hadj-Hammou et al., 2017).

Cheap to startBuilds community involvementNot continuous; misses night dischargesData quality varies

Option C

More frequent manual sampling

Inspectors visit outfalls more often with portable meters and send samples to a laboratory, the traditional method that wireless monitoring is designed to complement (Adu-Manu et al., 2017).

Accurate, legally acceptedUses existing staffLabour-heavy and costly to scaleStill hours to days behind

Option D

AI-assisted IoT monitoring

Connected sensors would feed an anomaly detection model that flags unusual readings for review. To compare this option, the team needs to define the model, the data needed to train and validate it, service costs and how operators would verify alerts.

Design selection

Comparing the pilot design with the alternatives

Each criterion is scored from 1 to 5. The weighted scores add to a total out of 100. The AI-assisted IoT option will receive a total once its scores are supported.

Figure 2. Weighted decision matrix. Each coloured block is one criterion’s contribution to the total.

Among the three scored options, sensor stations scored highest on timeliness and traceability. The implementation and cost sections address start-up costs and probe maintenance. The comparison with AI-assisted IoT remains open.

The proposed design retains two features from the scored alternatives. Residents can report pollution with a photo in the app, and inspectors can seek laboratory confirmation before enforcement.

Alert rules

When a station raises the alarm

Limits follow the national surface-water regulation QCVN 08:2023/BTNMT (Ministry of Natural Resources and Environment, 2023). During a two-week set-up, each station also learns its own baseline, so a river that is already polluted still shows sudden changes.

pH6.0–8.5

Outside this range is the lowest quality level, D.

Dissolved oxygen< 4 mg/L

Below the minimum for level C water.

Turbidity+50%

QCVN has no turbidity limit, so a sudden rise above baseline signals suspended solids.

TemperatureLogged

Used to correct pH and oxygen readings.

Community concerns

Questions residents asked, and the design’s answers

What if the alert is wrong?

Three readings in a row must break a limit, faulty probes are flagged, and alerts say “detected, being checked”. A laboratory sample confirms before any fine.

Will it be stolen or flooded?

The box is locked in a steel cage above the flood line, and ward staff help watch the site.

My parents do not use smartphones.

Alerts also go out by SMS and through ward loudspeaker announcements.

Does the app collect my data?

No account is needed. Location stays on the phone; only water data is published.

Could a factory tamper with it?

Readings are signed and kept as raw logs, and opening the box sends a tamper alert.

Limitation

The simulator tests the detection and alert logic, not sensor accuracy in real water. Oxygen probes need frequent calibration, and real outfalls bring fouling and floods, so a field pilot is recommended.

Rollout plan

Start small, prove it, then scale

Before operations

Prepare

  • Agree pilot sites and alert steps with the Department of Agriculture and Environment.
  • Get access from the drainage company.
  • Train inspectors and ward staff.
Year 1

Commission

  • Commission three stations at approved high-risk outfalls. The Nhuệ and Tô Lịch are candidate rivers.
  • Run a two-week baseline.
  • Launch the app with participating wards.
Years 1–3

Operate and evaluate

  • Compare investigated alerts with laboratory samples where available.
  • Measure detection time, false alarms and app use.
  • Review outcomes at the end of the pilot.
After year 3

Decide whether to scale

  • Use pilot results to assess expansion to industrial clusters.
  • Review the scope of a public data portal.
  • Confirm the operator and funding before expansion.

Recommendations

What should happen next

Run a field pilot with real sensors

Check probe accuracy, fouling and battery life at the first outfall before commissioning the other two pilot stations.

Add ammonium sensing

Ammonium is a direct sign of sewage, which the current four probes only show indirectly.

Agree the legal role of the data

Work with the city on how alerts and lab results are used as evidence.

Test with older residents

Check SMS and loudspeaker alerts with the people least likely to use the app.

Open the data

Publish quality-labelled data so researchers can study long-term trends.

Link to existing stations

Combine outfall data with the city’s river stations to trace pollution downstream.
